## 📝 Lecture Summary (At a Glance)

* **Definition of Elasticity:** Demand elasticity measures the *degree of responsiveness*—specifically, how much the quantity demanded changes when the price of a good or service changes.
* **Elastic vs. Inelastic:** "Elastic" means consumers are highly responsive to price changes (demand shifts significantly). "Inelastic" means consumers are generally unresponsive to price changes (demand stays relatively stable).
* **Determinants:** Elasticity is heavily influenced by the availability of substitutes, whether the good is a luxury or a necessity, the time horizon, and how broadly or narrowly the market is defined.
* **Measurement:** Elasticity is calculated as the ratio of the percentage change in quantity demanded to the percentage change in price.
* **Revenue Impact:** Understanding elasticity is critical for business strategy; raising prices on an elastic good will decrease total revenue, whereas raising prices on an inelastic good will increase total revenue.

## 1. What is Demand Elasticity?

When we talk about the Law of Demand, we know that when prices go up, demand goes down. However, **elasticity** asks a deeper question: *By exactly how much does the demand go down?*

* **Demand Elasticity** is the measure of how responsive or sensitive consumers are to a change in price.
* This concept allows businesses to predict how a price hike or a discount will impact their total sales volume.

## 2. Types of Demand Elasticity

Depending on the product, the market's response to price changes falls into different categories:

### Elastic Demand
* **Definition:** A relatively small change in price leads to a substantially larger change in the quantity demanded. Consumers are highly sensitive to price.
* **Graphical Representation:** The demand curve is flatter.
* *Example:* If Netflix raises its subscription price, many users might easily cancel their subscriptions because it is a non-essential service with other entertainment substitutes available.

### Inelastic Demand
* **Definition:** A change in price leads to a relatively smaller (or negligible) change in the quantity demanded. Consumers will keep buying the product regardless of the price hike.
* **Graphical Representation:** The demand curve is steeper.
* *Example:* Life-saving medications like insulin, or essential utilities like petrol. Even if the price increases significantly, consumers still need to buy them.

### Other Theoretical Types
* **Unit Elastic Demand:** The percentage change in quantity demanded is exactly equal to the percentage change in price (e.g., a 10% price drop leads to exactly a 10% demand increase). The ratio is exactly 1.
* **Perfectly Elastic Demand:** Even a tiny increase in price causes the quantity demanded to instantly drop to zero (often seen in perfectly commoditized markets with infinite identical substitutes). 
* **Perfectly Inelastic Demand:** The quantity demanded remains absolutely constant, completely irrespective of how high or low the price goes.

## 3. Determinants of Elasticity (What makes demand shift?)

Why are some goods highly elastic and others highly inelastic? It depends on several key market variables:

* **Availability of Substitutes:** If a product has many close competitors, its demand is more elastic because consumers can easily switch.
  * *Example:* If the price of one specific brand of butter goes up, consumers will just buy a different brand.
* **Nature of the Good (Necessity vs. Luxury):** Necessities (like healthcare or basic food) are highly **inelastic**. Luxuries (like designer clothes or high-end electronics) are highly **elastic**.
* **Market Definition:** Narrowly defined markets are more elastic than broadly defined ones.
  * *Example:* "Vanilla Ice Cream" is a narrowly defined market with high elasticity (you can easily swap it for chocolate ice cream). "Food" in general is broadly defined and highly inelastic (you cannot substitute eating).
* **Time Horizon:** Goods tend to become more elastic over a longer period of time because consumers have time to adapt and find alternatives.
  * *Example:* If petrol prices spike, demand is inelastic in the short term. However, over five years, people might switch to electric vehicles, making the long-term demand for petrol more elastic.

## 4. Measuring Elasticity mathematically

To make data-driven decisions, analysts measure elasticity using a specific ratio formula:

* **Formula:** `% Change in Quantity Demanded` divided by `% Change in Price`.
* **Interpreting the Ratio:**
  * **Ratio > 1:** The product is **Elastic** (Quantity changed more than Price).
  * **Ratio < 1:** The product is **Inelastic** (Quantity changed less than Price).
  * **Ratio = 1:** The product is **Unit Elastic**.

## 5. Elasticity and Total Revenue

Understanding elasticity is arguably the most important factor in a company's pricing strategy because it directly dictates **Total Revenue** (`Price * Quantity Sold`).

* **If Demand is Elastic (Ratio > 1):** 
  * Increasing the price will *decrease* total revenue (the loss of customers outweighs the higher price).
  * Decreasing the price will *increase* total revenue (the influx of new customers outweighs the discount).
* **If Demand is Inelastic (Ratio < 1):**
  * Increasing the price will *increase* total revenue (you lose very few customers, but make more money per sale).
  * Decreasing the price will *decrease* total revenue (you don't gain enough new customers to make up for the lower price).
