## 📝 Lecture Summary (At a Glance)
* **The Law of Demand and Supply** dictates market behavior, defining the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, and the direct relationship between price and quantity supplied.
* Distinguishing between a **movement along the curve** (caused by price changes) and a **shift in the curve** (caused by non-price factors) is critical for market analysis.
* **Market Equilibrium** occurs where the quantity supplied exactly equals the quantity demanded, representing the optimal price point without shortage or surplus.
* Real-world pricing strategies, such as setting premium ticket rates for sports events or determining telecommunications data tariffs, are directly governed by equilibrium principles.
* **Price Elasticity of Demand** measures consumer responsiveness to price changes, categorizing products as elastic, inelastic, or unit-elastic.
* Understanding elasticity is essential for maximizing **Total Revenue**, as price hikes only benefit revenue if the demand is inelastic.

## 1. The Law of Demand and Supply
* **Law of Demand:** States that, all else being equal, as the price of a good increases, the **Quantity Demanded** decreases, and vice versa. 
* **Law of Supply:** States that, all else being equal, as the price of a good increases, the **Quantity Supplied** increases.
* **Movement Along the Curve:** Occurs strictly when the *price* of the good changes. This changes the quantity demanded or supplied, but not the overall demand/supply relationship.
* **Shift in the Curve:** Occurs when *non-price determinants* change (e.g., consumer income, preferences, technology, or input costs). This creates an entirely new demand or supply curve.

## 2. Applied Market Economics: Real-World Case Studies
* The professor utilized contemporary examples to demonstrate how macroeconomic and microeconomic forces shift demand and supply curves.
* *Example: The 5G Ecosystem.* The introduction of 5G infrastructure impacts both the demand for compatible smartphones and the pricing strategies for data tariffs. As technology improves (a non-price determinant), the supply curve for data services shifts, while consumer preference for faster speeds shifts the demand curve for 5G devices.
* *Example: Live Market Gold Pricing.* Gold prices fluctuate constantly based on global supply constraints and shifts in investor demand (often driven by external economic stability), demonstrating rapid shifts in market equilibrium.

## 3. Market Equilibrium
* **Market Equilibrium:** The specific price point where the **Demand Curve** and **Supply Curve** intersect. At this point, **Quantity Demanded** equals **Quantity Supplied** ($Q_d = Q_s$).
* **Surplus:** Occurs when the price is set *above* the equilibrium point, resulting in the quantity supplied exceeding the quantity demanded. Prices naturally fall to resolve a surplus.
* **Shortage:** Occurs when the price is set *below* the equilibrium point, resulting in the quantity demanded exceeding the quantity supplied. Prices naturally rise to resolve a shortage.
* *Example: IPL Cricket Match Ticket Pricing.* When organizers price premium seats, they must estimate the equilibrium point. If priced too high, seats remain empty (Surplus). If priced too low, they sell out instantly, leaving potential revenue on the table (Shortage). The goal is to find the exact price that clears the market.

## 4. Price Elasticity of Demand
* **Price Elasticity of Demand ($E_d$):** A measurement of how sensitive the quantity demanded is to a change in price. 
* Calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price: $E_d = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \Delta P}$
* **Inelastic Demand ($E_d < 1$):** Consumers are relatively unresponsive to price changes. A price increase leads to a smaller percentage drop in quantity demanded. (Often essential goods).
* **Elastic Demand ($E_d > 1$):** Consumers are highly responsive to price changes. A small price increase leads to a large percentage drop in quantity demanded. (Often luxury goods or items with many substitutes).
* **Unit-Elastic Demand ($E_d = 1$):** The percentage change in price results in an identical percentage change in quantity demanded.

## 5. Elasticity and Total Revenue
* **Total Revenue (TR):** Calculated as Price multiplied by Quantity Sold ($TR = P \times Q$).
* The relationship between price changes and total revenue depends entirely on elasticity:
    * If demand is **Inelastic**, raising prices *increases* total revenue.
    * If demand is **Elastic**, raising prices *decreases* total revenue.
    * If demand is **Unit-Elastic**, price changes do not affect total revenue.
