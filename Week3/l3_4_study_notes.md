## 📝 Lecture Summary (At a Glance)

* **Organizational Buying Behavior** refers to the specific process by which B2B firms make purchasing decisions.
* Unlike B2C transactions, B2B purchases involve **zero impulse buying** because of the exceptionally high purchase value and severe financial risks involved.
* A B2B purchase is typically triggered by one of two conditions: when the company recognizes a **problem** (e.g., poor equipment performance) or identifies a new **need**.
* B2B purchasing is a highly structured, formal process that typically spans **8 distinct steps**, ranging from problem recognition to post-usage performance reviews.
* Because the financial stakes are so high (often millions of dollars), decisions are not made by individuals, but rather by a **Decision Making Unit (DMU)** or Buying Center.
* **Supplier Selection** is made using an objective rating system based on weighted **Key Success Factors (KSFs)** or **Critical Success Factors (CSFs)** like price, brand image, service quality, and flexibility.

## 1. B2C vs. B2B Buying Behavior

### B2C (Business-to-Consumer) Buying
* **Impulse Buying:** B2C purchases are frequently unplanned and spontaneous. 
  * *Example: Going to a shopping mall on a Saturday, seeing a nice t-shirt, and deciding to buy it on the spot without prior planning.*

### B2B (Business-to-Business) Buying
* **No Impulse Buying:** B2B purchases are always premeditated.
* **High Purchase Value:** Transactions involve millions to billions of dollars/euros, necessitating careful deliberation.
* **Long Purchase Duration:** Decisions can take anywhere from 3 months to a year. 
* **High Jilting Impact:** Canceling an order causes severe financial damage to the supplier.

## 2. Triggers for a B2B Purchase

A B2B purchase process is initiated strictly under two conditions:
1. **When there is a problem:** The current supplier or equipment is underperforming.
   * *Example: Company engineers complain that their current laptops are slow and have a poor battery life.*
2. **When there is a need:** The company has a requirement for new solutions or technology.

## 3. The 8-Step B2B Buying Process

B2B organizational buying is a formal process. While it can vary slightly between companies, it generally follows these 8 steps:

### Step 1: Problem Recognition
* The **Decision Making Unit (DMU)** (or users within the company) identify a problem or a need.
  * *Example: Engineers complain about their laptops being slow and dying quickly, identifying a need for high-performance laptops.*

### Step 2: General Description of Needs
* The company outlines the broad requirements needed to solve the problem.
  * *Example: The engineers state they need laptops that are faster and have a long battery life.*

### Step 3: Product Specifications (Product Specs)
* The general needs are translated into strict, technical requirements.
* *Example: Translating "fast and good battery" into specific specs: Intel Core i7 processor or above, 13-inch screen for portability, 9 hours of battery life on a single charge, and 16GB of RAM.*

### Step 4: Supplier Search
* The purchase department conducts market research to identify potential suppliers capable of meeting the product specifications.
  * *Example: Creating a shortlist of potential laptop vendors: HP, Lenovo, Dell, and Apple.*

### Step 5: Analysis and Acquisition of Proposals
* The company sends out a **Request for Quote (RFQ)** or Request for Proposal (RFP) to the shortlisted vendors.
* The vendors submit detailed quotes outlining their prices, services, and how they meet the specifications.

### Step 6: Supplier Selection
* The DMU evaluates the proposals using a weighted matrix.
* They identify **Critical Success Factors (CSFs)** or Key Success Factors (KSFs) (e.g., Price, Brand Image, Service Quality, Flexibility).
* Each factor is assigned a weight (totaling 100%).
* Each supplier is rated on a scale (e.g., 1 to 5) for each factor.
* **Final Score:** The rating is multiplied by the weight to get a final score. The supplier with the highest total score wins the contract.
  * *Example: Dell might win the contract over Apple, HP, and Lenovo based on a combination of a lower price, decent brand image, and high service quality.*

### Step 7: Selection of Order Routine
* The buyer and the chosen supplier define the logistical details: delivery schedules, payment terms, warranties, and maintenance agreements.
  * *Example: Specifying that 50% of the laptops are paid for in cash upfront, with the remaining 50% paid in installments, along with the delivery dates.*

### Step 8: Post-Usage Performance Review
* After the product/service is delivered and used, the buying company evaluates whether the supplier met expectations.
* If expectations are met, the company will likely repurchase from them in the future. If not, the company will switch suppliers, restarting the 8-step process.
