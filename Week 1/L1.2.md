## 1. Defining Demand
While human wants and desires are infinite, they do not automatically translate to economic demand. 
* **Demand** is a desire that is specifically backed by the **ability and willingness to pay**.
* *Example:* If a person from a low-income group desires a luxury car but lacks the purchasing power, it is merely a desire, not an economic demand.

## 2. Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics
Economics is broadly divided into two lenses through which we view resource allocation and decision-making:

### Microeconomics ("Small")
* **Focus:** Individual consumers, specific businesses, and smaller parts of the economy.
* **Approach:** Bottom-up. It looks at the financial decisions of individuals and firms.
* **Examples:** 
  * How an individual allocates their time (work vs. leisure).
  * How an IPL (Indian Premier League) cricket franchise builds a team while dealing with constraints like budget caps and squad size limits.

### Macroeconomics ("Large")
* **Focus:** The economy as a whole.
* **Approach:** Top-down. It looks at aggregate metrics and societal-level impacts.
* **Examples:**
  * Government budget allocations (e.g., spending more on education means less for infrastructure).
  * The impact of geopolitical events or broad policies (like tariffs) on national inflation, GDP, and the employment rate.

## 3. The Relationship Between Households and Firms
The economy functions on a dependency loop between individuals (households) and businesses (firms):
1. **Individuals** sell their labor, time, and skills to firms in exchange for money (wages).
2. **Firms** are dependent on this labor to produce goods and services.
3. **Firms** then sell these goods and services back to the individuals to generate profit.

## 4. The Three Fundamental Concepts of Economics
The entire field of economics is rooted in three core concepts: Scarcity, Utility, and Marginality.

### A. Scarcity
Scarcity is the imbalance between unlimited human wants and finite resources. There are three main causes of scarcity:
1. **Demand-Induced Scarcity:** Occurs when the population grows or a product becomes highly popular, driving demand up while supply stays the same.
2. **Supply-Induced Scarcity:** Occurs when the supply of a resource drops drastically (e.g., crop failures due to bad weather, or fuel shortages due to geopolitical tensions).
3. **Structural Scarcity:** Occurs when there is unequal access to resources (e.g., people in rural areas having less access to healthcare or amenities compared to those in Tier-1 urban cities).

### B. Utility
* **Definition:** The satisfaction or pleasure a consumer derives from consuming a good or service.
* **Context matters:** Utility influences economic decisions heavily. For instance, the utility of buying a heavy coat is high for someone in a colder climate but very low for someone in a warmer climate.

### C. Marginality
* **Definition:** The incremental or step-by-step changes in economic decisions. People rarely make all-or-nothing decisions; they make decisions "at the margin" (bit by bit). 
* **Marginal Utility:** The specific, incremental increase in satisfaction derived from consuming just *one additional unit* of a good.

## 5. The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility
A crucial rule of consumer behavior stating that **as consumption increases, the marginal utility (satisfaction from each additional unit) decreases.**
* *Example:* If you are hungry, eating the first slice of pizza (or ice cream) gives you immense satisfaction (high utility). The second slice gives you slightly less satisfaction. By the fifth or sixth slice, you might feel sick, meaning the marginal utility has become negative. 
* **Key Assumptions for this law:** The items consumed must be identical, consumed quickly in one sitting, and the consumer must have constant tastes and behave rationally.

## 6. Opportunity Cost
Because of scarcity, every choice involves a trade-off. 
* **Definition:** The cost of the *next best alternative* that is given up when a choice is made. 
* *Example 1:* If you spend your money on a new backpack, the opportunity cost might be the groceries you could have bought at the supermarket with that same money.
* *Example 2:* If you spend your time studying, the opportunity cost is the leisure or sleep you sacrificed.
